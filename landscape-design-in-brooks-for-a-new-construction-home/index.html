What should landscape design for a new home cover?

Landscape design is the process of planning how the outdoor areas around a home will look and work. For a new construction home, the plan should consider the house entrance, paths, outdoor living areas, lawn, planting beds, trees, and how water moves across the property. It should also show how those parts fit together, rather than treating each request as a separate add-on.

A useful plan can guide installation in phases if you are not ready to complete every part at once. It may include a layout or drawing, plant and material selections, and notes about preparation and installation. Ask what drawings and specifications are included, since the level of detail varies by provider and project.

What does a local professional check before designing?

A site visit helps the designer understand conditions that may not be clear from a floor plan or builder rendering. The house position, finished grades, sun and shade, access for equipment, and existing trees can all affect the layout. On new construction sites, the professional should also ask what work the builder has completed and what is still planned.

  • Where water drains now, and whether the final grading plan or downspout locations affect proposed beds and lawn.
  • Where utility lines, irrigation components, septic features, or other protected areas are located, if applicable.
  • Which areas need a path or firm access, and where you want privacy, shade, play space, or room for gatherings.
  • How much direct sun different parts of the yard receive, which informs plant and lawn choices.
  • Whether construction traffic has left compacted soil or debris that needs attention before planting or sod installation.

The design should be based on the final site conditions, not only on assumptions made before construction is complete. If grading or utility work is unfinished, confirm what can be designed now and what should wait for a follow-up check.

Which design choices make sense for your household?

Begin with how you will use the property. A household that wants open space for children or pets may prioritize lawn and clear routes, while someone who entertains may want a defined patio area and a comfortable connection between the house and yard. Planting beds, shade trees, and screening can be added around those priorities.

You can also decide whether to complete the work at once or establish a clear first phase. For example, the first stage might address drainage and access, with larger planting areas or decorative details planned for later. Keeping a complete layout from the start can help future work fit the original design.

What drives the cost of landscape design and installation?

The price depends on what you are asking the provider to plan and build. A design-only engagement differs from a project that includes site preparation, planting, sod, paths, or other installation. The size and shape of the usable yard, access for equipment, changes needed after construction, and the materials and plants selected can also affect the estimate.

Ask for design work and installation to be described separately, even if one provider handles both. Confirm whether soil preparation, removal of construction debris, grading adjustments, delivery, and cleanup are included or excluded. If you are phasing the work, request a plan that identifies what each stage covers so you can compare proposals on the same basis.

What can change the project timeline?

Scheduling depends on when the home and yard are ready, the design review process, the scope of work, and the provider's availability. Wet or unsuitable ground, unfinished grading, utility work, or changes requested after approval can affect when installation can begin. Plant and material availability may also influence sequencing.

Before setting a start date, coordinate with the builder and confirm which tasks must be finished first. Agree on the order of work, how weather delays are handled, and who will let you know if the schedule changes. A realistic timeline is more useful than a date that assumes every site condition is already settled.

What mistakes should you avoid on a new construction landscape?

  • Installing sod or plants before confirming finished grading and drainage.
  • Choosing plants or lawn areas without checking sun exposure, soil condition, and intended use.
  • Starting installation without confirming the location of utilities and other site features.
  • Using the entire budget on visible details while leaving necessary preparation or access unresolved.
  • Making separate purchases or hiring separate crews without a shared layout and clear responsibility for the handoff.

A common source of frustration is treating the yard as a final cosmetic task after every other decision has been made. Even if installation will happen later, an early conversation can help preserve access, confirm dependencies, and avoid redoing finished work.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What does your landscape design service include, and what drawings or selections will I receive?
  • Will you visit the property and review the final grades before preparing the plan?
  • Who handles installation, and which parts of the work are included in your proposal?
  • How do you coordinate with a builder if construction or site work is still underway?
  • What preparation, drainage-related work, debris removal, and cleanup are included or excluded?
  • Can the design be installed in phases without disrupting the overall layout?
  • How are changes, delays, and additional work documented and approved?

A good answer should be specific to your property and easy to match to the written proposal. If an item is unclear, ask for it to be explained before you approve the scope.

When should you call a professional, and what is the next step?

Call a landscape professional while construction is underway if you need help coordinating yard decisions with the builder, or before installation if you are unsure about drainage, grading, plant placement, or the scope of work. For local rules, ask which jurisdiction applies to your property and verify requirements for any proposed structures, grading, drainage changes, or protected features with the relevant local office. Requirements can depend on the address and the work involved.

The next step is to gather your site plan, builder's information about grading and utilities, and a short list of how you want to use the yard. Then arrange a site discussion with Hearn Landscape or another provider and ask for a written scope that separates design, preparation, and installation.
