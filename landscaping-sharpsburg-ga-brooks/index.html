What should landscaping include?

Landscaping usually means planning or changing the planted areas and outdoor layout of a property. Depending on the goal, the work may involve design, preparing planting beds, installing plants, or supplying and laying sod. It is different from routine mowing and other recurring lawn upkeep.

Before asking for a proposal, write down what you want the space to do. You may be refreshing the front entry, replacing a patchy lawn, improving how an outdoor area is used, or making the yard easier to care for. A clear goal helps a provider describe the work that belongs in the project and what falls outside it.

What does a local professional check first?

A useful site review starts with the property itself, not a plant list. A professional may look at the existing lawn and beds, sunlight, slopes, drainage patterns, soil condition, access for materials, and any features that need to stay in place. Those details can affect what will work and how much preparation is needed.

Be clear about any trouble spots you have noticed, such as water collecting after rain or plants that have struggled in a particular area. Ask the provider to explain how those conditions shape the recommendation. If the address is in Brooks but you searched for Sharpsburg, confirm the service area using the project address rather than relying on the search wording alone.

Which landscaping approach fits your project?

A focused update can make sense when most of the yard works well and one area needs attention. A broader redesign may be more appropriate when you want several parts of the property to feel connected or when existing features need to be considered together.

  • Bed refresh: useful for updating planting areas while leaving the rest of the landscape in place.
  • Planting plan: helps organize plant choices and placement around the conditions of the site and the look you want.
  • Sod project: may suit a lawn replacement or a new lawn area, depending on site preparation and access.
  • Combined project: can coordinate design, beds, plants, and sod when several parts of the yard need work.

Ask which tasks are included in the recommendation and which would require separate work. That distinction helps you compare proposals on the same basis.

What affects the cost of landscaping?

There is no dependable price without knowing the property and the requested work. Cost can change with the size of the area, how much existing material needs removal, site access, preparation needs, the amount and type of materials, and whether the project includes design as well as installation.

Drainage concerns, slopes, or difficult access may call for extra planning or labor. A proposal should make clear what is included, what materials are covered, and what conditions could lead to a change in scope. If you are comparing bids, check that each one describes similar work instead of comparing totals alone.

How can you plan the timing?

The schedule depends on the project scope, the provider's availability, site preparation, and the timing of materials. A small, straightforward update may take less coordination than a design-led project with several stages, but the provider needs to review the site before giving you a realistic timeline.

Ask when the work could start, how long each stage is expected to take, and what could affect the schedule. Weather and material availability can change plans, so find out how the provider will communicate delays or proposed adjustments.

What mistakes should you avoid?

  • Choosing from a total price without checking the written scope, preparation, and materials.
  • Assuming landscaping includes mowing, cleanup, irrigation work, or other tasks that are not listed in the proposal.
  • Requesting a finished look without explaining which existing plants, features, or usable areas should remain.
  • Skipping a discussion about drainage or other site problems that may affect the result.
  • Treating a search location as proof that a provider serves your exact address.

A written proposal and a clear conversation about site conditions can prevent mismatched expectations. If an important detail is missing, ask for it to be clarified before agreeing to the work.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Do you serve my property address in Brooks?
  • What work is included in the proposal, and what is excluded?
  • Who will handle design, preparation, and installation?
  • What site conditions could change the scope or schedule?
  • Which materials are included, and how are material choices approved?
  • What is the expected start window and sequence of work?
  • How will you tell me about delays, changes, or additional work?
  • Are there local rules, approvals, or property restrictions I should confirm before work begins?

For rules and approvals, do not assume every property has the same requirements. Ask the provider what they know about the project, then confirm any applicable requirements with the relevant local office or property association.

When should you call a professional?

Consider a professional when the project covers several areas, you want a coordinated design, you are replacing a lawn, or you are unsure how a site condition should affect the plan. A site review can help turn a general idea into a scope you can compare and schedule.

For a simple task, you may only need a focused proposal. For a larger update, get the design intent, preparation, materials, and installation responsibilities clear before work starts.
